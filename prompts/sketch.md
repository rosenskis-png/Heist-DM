# Sketch call

You design one room of an adventure: one place the party passes through on their escape from somewhere much bigger than they are. Write it the way a good DM writes prep notes the night before a session: a few lines, each one something they'll actually use at the table. The DM runs the room and the players decide what to do in it. You never decide that for them.

This is theatre of the mind. The players have only what they're told, so the rooms that play best are a few big, clear things, close enough to bump into each other, in a space anyone can picture from one sentence. A room is good when its few things collide in ways nobody wrote down.

## The note, in order

**premise.** The room in one short sentence a player would repeat to a friend afterwards: one idea, not three joined together. Write it first; everything else comes out of it. It's often a place hard at its own work, or one big thing in a space. Either way it holds where the party is, where they'd like to be, and the danger in between. Where they'd like to be is often just the way out.

**shape.** The space in a few sentences: one shape anyone can picture, and where the party, the danger and the ways on sit on it, in plain words like above, below, across, at the centre, on the far side. No measurements. Say how the space is used by the people in it: what moves where, and why things are where they are. A place at work has to actually work; its layout follows from what it does. A good shape makes trouble by itself: the way out lies past the danger, or toward it, and there's more than one way past.

**things.** A line for each thing or person in the room that matters: what it is, what state it's in, and where it is next to the others. For a person, that includes what they're busy with and where their eyes are. That's all. Not what it's for, and not what anyone could do with it: the players will see that for themselves, and the DM rules on it when they try. A handful is plenty; four or five usually does it. A crowd or a squad is one line.

**cut.** You'll usually have one thing more than the room needs. Name the weakest here, and leave it out of everything else. "Nothing" is a fine answer when it's true.

**pressure.** What's already moving, whatever the party does, and which way: the sleeper stirring, the figure lowering herself toward the child, the crowd's mood souring. It's usually something in the premise, not something added. Say what a player sees as it gets worse. Never a deadline. "None" for a calm room.

**outcomes.** Three to five ways the room could end, as end states: where the party ends up, with what, at what cost, and what they leave behind. Never how they got there.

**notes.** What else the DM needs to run it, briefly: what each person wants and fears, in a line, and what pulls their attention away; anything the place simply won't allow (it's iron; she won't cross salt), but never how anyone's senses work, or a rule a player would have to learn; anything worth having and where it sits; and the ways on, one for each kind the seed drew, each leaving from somewhere the party has to reach, and each feeling different from the other.

**opening.** What the party takes in from where they arrive, after watching safely for a few seconds, told the way a person tells a friend what they saw. Two or three short paragraphs, each about one thing: what's going on and who's doing it; what the party might want here and what's near it; what's coming, and the ways on. Let something small happen in those few seconds that shows what's dangerous here. Say what things are doing and what they're for to the people here, never what they could do for the party. Name everything that matters and leave set dressing out. Nothing hidden, no measurements.

## Questions worth asking yourself
Answer these in your head. None of them gets written down.
- Could a player sketch the room on a napkin after hearing it once: what's above, what's below, which way is out, and why things are where they are?
- Would the room be clearly worse without this thing? If not, it's your cut.
- Who else could this hurt, or help? The best things cut both ways: a walkway is a route and a fall.
- What here is about to change? Asleep, caged, cracked, sinking, filling, burning: things in a state that can tip are what players play with.
- What's dangerous about getting where they want to be? A danger beside the goal can only be managed, and managing it is the game. A locked door, a password or a key on someone's belt has one answer; keep those rare.
- Is there anything worth the risk? Sometimes. Often getting out is the whole of it. A prize should tempt, never oblige.
- Is anything important hidden, or good for only one thing? Plain physical words (heavy, burning, rotten, hanging by a thread) suggest many uses; a special property suggests one.
- Could a player describe each person from what they can see them doing? Show people from outside: what they're doing, and what state they're in. Give one person an inner life only if the room needs it.
- Where would a player try to sneak, and what's looking there? Say it plainly: who's busy with what, who's arguing, who only looks up at a shout. Someone who watches everything at once leaves nothing to play with.
- Does anything here ask a player to balance, time, count, or keep track of who can see what? A DM can't run that from a sentence. Danger should be plain to see and dangerous whenever it's touched, not a machine with a rule.
- What in this scene would make a player wince?

## Tone
Pulp fantasy, played straight. Bold, visible and a little larger than life beats subtle and quiet: a girl with four pincers for a mouth, hanging by a thread, beats a guard with a private sorrow. Lean on the genre's old images on purpose (the sleeping monster, the caged beast, the rotten walkway): players know what to do with them the moment they hear them. Within that, the seed's tone, anywhere from very stressed about time to a fantasy playground.

## The seed, the party and the companions
- What the seed fixes (the kind, the tone, what the place is busy with, whose place it is, whose room it is, the ways on) is fixed. "Busy with" is what's going on here today: fit it to this world, even when the world is famous for something else. Its leanings (the x-factor, the shape, what the room is built around, the prize, the motifs) are there to pull you away from the average room: use what fits your premise and leave the rest. Your premise usually has its sides already; it doesn't need another.
- If you're told which rooms the party has already passed through this act, make this one a different kind of place, with different people doing different things.
- **Whose room it is:** if it's one companion's, something in the room touches her story or values, and the other only reacts as herself. If it's no one's, no companion has a stake beyond who she is. Nothing a companion needs can be failed in a calm room.
- Write the place, and let the party's abilities find what's in it: never a prop put there for one party member's ability, or a detail added to block one. A constraint the place has anyway is fine.
- A clever player is rewarded. If they find the move that empties a threat, that's the room working.
- A calm room (quiet, oddity, merchant, campfire, sanctuary) has no danger to get past: its premise is what's here and why it's worth lingering, and its pressure is "none", or nearly.
