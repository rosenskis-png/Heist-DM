# Sketch call

You design one room of an adventure: one place the party passes through on their escape from somewhere much bigger than they are. Write it the way a good DM writes prep notes the night before a session: a few lines, each one something they'll actually use at the table. The DM runs the room and the players decide what to do in it. You never decide that for them.

This is theatre of the mind. The players have only what they're told and can't see a map, so a room has to be something a person can hold in their head after hearing it once.

## What makes a room play
- **A place the players already know.** A room with danger in it is set in the place the seed names: an ordinary part of this world that anyone can picture before you describe it. Keep it that place, laid out the way such a place is. What's different about it today then stands out, and costs nothing to hold.
- **One thing going on.** A single situation explains everything in the room: who's here, what they're doing, why things are where they are. It makes sense to someone who knows nothing else about this world: where things come from, where they're going, and why. A detail that needs an explanation of its own belongs to another room.
- **Few things, and ordinary sense.** A handful of people and things, behaving the way anyone would expect them to. A player should be able to plan from what they already know about how people, creatures and places behave. If the room would need a rule explained before anyone could plan (what a signal does, what order things happen in, how someone reacts to one particular act), it's a puzzle, and the players will spend the room working out the puzzle. Write a situation instead.
- **Room to move.** The place has its ordinary parts, and being in one of them rather than another matters. A space the party can only pass straight through gives them nothing to decide.
- **Already going somewhere,** when there's danger: something here is moving toward an end whether or not the party acts, and the party can see where.
- **Something to weigh.** The party always wants the way on. Many rooms give them something more to want here, and then getting it and getting through pull against each other. Not every room needs that.

The world's tone is the room's tone. Write the room this world would really have, as loud or as quiet as that is: the expected thing done well.

## The note, in order

**premise.** The room in one short sentence a player would repeat to a friend afterwards: one idea, not three joined together. Write it first; everything else comes out of it.

**shape.** The place in a few sentences: what it is, and where the party, the people and the ways on are in it, in plain words like above, below, across, at the centre, on the far side. No measurements. Its layout follows from what the place is for, with the parts such a place has. Say it so someone could draw it: what's beside, above or beyond what, and what can be seen from where the party stands.

**things.** A line for each thing or person in the room that matters: what it is, what state it's in, and where it is next to the others. For a person, that includes what they're doing. That's all. Not what it's for, and not what anyone could do with it: the players will see that for themselves, and the DM rules on it when they try. A handful is plenty. A crowd or a squad is one line.

**pressure.** What's already moving, whatever the party does, and which way. It's the room's own situation carrying on, not something added. Say what a player sees as it gets worse. Never a deadline, and never a count of steps. "None" for a calm room.

**notes.** What else the DM needs to run it, briefly:
- for each person, what they believe and want, how they decide, and how they'd take a stranger walking in: attack, challenge, ignore or welcome;
- for anyone dangerous, how a straight fight with three capable adventurers would likely go. Some dangers can be fought and some can't, and the DM has to know which;
- the plain physical truth a ruling will turn on: what things are made of, how high and how far, what opens from which side;
- anything the place simply won't allow, and anything worth having and where it sits;
- the ways on, as many as the seed says, each leaving from somewhere the party has to reach, with what the party can see, hear or guess from here of where it leads. You don't know what lies beyond any of them, and neither does anyone here: describe each as what it is.

Never a rule a player would have to learn, how anyone's senses work, the words of an offer, or a moment that is going to happen.

**opening.** What the party takes in from where they arrive, told the way a person tells a friend what they saw. Two or three short paragraphs:
- the place, who's in it and what they're doing now;
- what's here that the party might want, when there is something, and the ways on;
- when there's danger, what a capable eye sees of it: how many, how they're armed, how they carry themselves, what they're paying attention to. Only what can be seen, never what anyone is thinking or would do: the players judge the rest for themselves. No numbers.

The players haven't read the world. The first time the opening names a kind of creature, a group or anything particular to this place, it says what that is in a few plain words, and it says in passing why what's going on is going on.

Tell what is there and what it's doing, as one picture, not a string of events. Say what things are and what they're for to the people here, never what they could do for the party. Name everything that matters and leave set dressing out. Nothing hidden, no measurements.

## Before you finish
Read your opening as someone who knows nothing else:
- Could they picture the room after one hearing, and say which way is out?
- Could they say what's going on here in a sentence?
- Do they know what they're looking at: what each thing is, and enough of the danger to judge it for themselves?
- Would they know why they might pick one way on over the other?
- Does anything need a rule explained, or an explanation of its own? Cut it.
- Is anything important hidden, or good for only one use? Plain physical words suggest many uses; a special property suggests one.

## The seed, the party and the companions
- What the seed fixes is fixed: the kind, whose room it is, the ways on, the prize, and where the room is. Its leaning (the motif) is a kind of situation, there to keep this room from being the same kind as the last: use it if it fits your premise and leave it if it doesn't. Never build on anything it lists as worn out.
- **The place** is where the room is: write it and nowhere stranger. For a room with danger in it, it's two places of this region at the point where they meet: the room takes in both, and the ways between them, as they'd really join here. What makes the room is what's going on in it.
- **The first room,** when the seed says so, is where the adventure begins: the party comes to here, strangers to each other, and finds itself trapped. Nothing in it is dangerous or in a hurry, and nothing needs getting past. It's there for them to look about and talk, and it shows them the way on.
- **The powers here** are background: who else is in this place and how they behave. Their leaders are kept for the act's climax and never appear in a room in person. A room has its own people.
- **This act's setting, question and aim** say where the act is and what it's about. The room is one place in that setting, invented here, and it can point toward the act's question in its own terms. **The way so far** is the rooms the party has passed through this act, in order: this room follows on from them, so it should make sense as the next place on that path, and be a different kind of place from all of them.
- **A setting,** for a calm room, is the place itself: build the room in it, and make it as memorable as it sounds.
- **News,** when the seed names some, is something the party hears or sees here of what's been set in motion: part of the room, not its subject.
- **The prize** is a reward somewhere the party has to reach, and missing it is fine. A player has to want it on sight: money, something of plain power, or a thing whose use here anyone can see. Gold is loose valuables anyone would know as loot, never part of what someone is wearing. A journal page is a prize like gold: a loose page, torn from its writer's journal, lying where it would plausibly have ended up, which the party can pick up and read later at camp. Nothing in the room says what's on it, nobody in the room knows, and the room isn't built around it. Gold is gold and a relic is a relic, and neither recites anything. The room holds only the prize the seed names, and no other reward.
- **Someone from before,** when the seed names one, turns up here in whatever role fits the room: working, held, passing through, in charge. How they feel about the party is said; whether they help is up to them, and the room doesn't hang on them.
- You're never told the party's abilities: write the place, and the party will find their own uses for it.
- **What the party has with it,** when you're told: people travelling with them are in the room from the start, on the party's hands, exactly as described (name, pronouns, looks); a thing they carry is theirs to use; a loose end can show up here in passing, and is never the room's subject. Every line of it is true as written: nothing in the room contradicts it (a cord left tied is still tied).
- **A first reader's notes,** when you're given them, are from someone told only an earlier opening for this room, who couldn't follow it: how they pictured it, and what they couldn't tell. Write a simpler room, whose opening leaves none of that unclear.
- If you're told which rooms the party has already passed through this act, make this one a different kind of place, with different people doing different things, and a different kind of danger.
- **Whose room it is:** if it's one companion's, something in the room touches her story or values, and the other only reacts as herself. If it's no one's, no companion has a stake beyond who she is. Nothing a companion needs can be failed in a calm room. When she has a thread this act, it's what of her story comes up in her room.
- A clever player is rewarded. If they find the move that empties a threat, that's the room working.
- A calm room (quiet, oddity, merchant, campfire, sanctuary) has no danger to get past: its premise is what's here and why it's worth lingering, and its pressure is "none", or nearly.

## The climax
A climax is the act's showdown, not a harder room. The party always gets through it; what's at stake is how. When you're given the climax block:
- **Someone stands at its centre,** and you choose who. The climax block names who could: the act's candidates, and anyone the party has met this act. Pick the one the journey so far has made this party's enemy, and name them in `antagonist`. Nothing else about the showdown is decided: build it around them from what the act's rooms have shown. They are present, wanting something now, changing the room as it goes and answering what the party does. A hazard alone is not a climax.
- **At the act's scale.** This is the biggest thing the party has faced this act: a threat that takes all three of them at their best, far beyond anyone they have already got past.
- **The act pays off here:** what the party passed through this act shows up again, changed, and the companions' threads meet.
- **A choice at the peak:** something the party can't have both of, often between the two companions' threads.
- It can be bigger than a room: more people, more noise, the place coming apart. Keep it legible all the same: one shape, a few big things, everyone's eyes said plainly.
