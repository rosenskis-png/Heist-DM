# Decisions archive

Entries from `docs/decisions.md` that now apply only as history: wholly superseded, or describing the engine pipeline the DM's rulings replaced (D125, D129). Each is moved word for word and never edited (D145). Its heading stays in the log with a pointer here. Read these to trace why something was tried and dropped, not to learn the current design.

---

### D16. A step takes place in the tick it starts in
**Decided:** A committed plan's first step happens on the current beat, and the tick advances when a step ends.
**Why:** The spec never said this, and the Cells walkthrough contradicted itself. Under this rule, Card 2 and Lyra's "right now it's the cask" are right. Card 1 has been corrected in `fixtures/cells/walkthrough.md`.

### D18. Where engine reactions stop and the world call starts
**Decided:** A reaction is an engine rule if it can be written as condition → operations with no "unless he decides". Anything that chooses between options (how Snag bargains, whether he fights) goes to the world call.

### D19. Distance and senses, minimal
**Decided:** Travel cost is in ticks (D9's geometry adds exact distances for the AIs as facts). Senses are see and hear per schedule beat, plus a light level per zone. Smell, and chase calibration by length, wait for the first encounter that needs them.

### D25. Hand-author Act 1 before building the generator
**Decided:** Act 1's packets are written by hand (with Claude's help) and serve as the quality bar for the generator in Phase 7.
**Why:** It keeps "does the loop work?" and "does the generator work?" from being tested at the same time.
**Not in conflict with "Remove the DM's judgment":** the runtime treats a packet the same whoever wrote it, and the judgment still happens in rulings and texture at play time. What that reflex rejects is replacing judgment with lookup tables.

### D29. The map is complete: no improvised structure
**Decided:** No AI call can add a zone, a connection or a hidden thing during play. A feature the packet doesn't have doesn't exist: the step call rules it out ("is there a coal chute?" "No."). A hidden feature the packet does have is used and revealed normally. Texture (a candelabra) and rulings about existing things ("one rat can drag the ring") are unchanged.
**Why:** The designer's call (2026-10-30): start with none, and loosen it later for player-initiated or DM-initiated features if playtests want it. It keeps the map fixed while the loop is proven.
**Rules out:** An `add_connection` or `add_zone` operation, for now.

### D31. Encounter clocks leave room for one recovered failure
**Decided:** An encounter's deadline should fit some exploring plus a plan in which one key roll fails and the recovery is handled well. Two careless failures shouldn't fit. The Cells start at tick 0 on Snag's Drink beat, and Uzga arrives at tick 18 (a starting value; playtests move it).
**Why:** Half of the heist fantasy is the mid-heist adjustment, or the smooth recovery from something that almost blew it. A deadline only perfect play can meet is a puzzle. The old numbers also implied the encounter started at tick 2 without saying so.

### D34. A card shows problems instead of pricing the impossible
**Decided:** The engine projects each step as if the earlier steps' problems were fixed, so one problem doesn't flag every later step. A step with a problem of its own (the keys aren't on the peg at that tick; a trigger fires first) shows the problem and isn't sent for pricing.
**Why:** Card 1 priced walking the party past Snag after the engine had already projected him going for the drum: odds on a step that can't happen as written. The card's job is to surface every timing problem at once (`fixtures/cells/walkthrough.md` §6), and a problem says that better than a long-shot price.

### D37. Deadlines leave room for two recovered failures
**Decided:** Supersedes the sizing in D31. An encounter's deadline should fit some exploring plus a plan in which two key rolls fail and both recoveries are handled well. Uzga now arrives at tick 21 (a starting value; playtests move it).
**Why:** With no warnings on the card (D36), more plans fail in the fiction, so recovery needs more room. A failure must still leave a new situation, never a dead end.

### D39. Someone in the party has to perceive a condition
**Decided:** A plan's `when` or `if` is checked only if a party member can see or hear what it's about at that moment. The whisper rings make sharing free, so it can be anyone in the party. If no one can, the plan is interrupted there (`cant_perceive`): "I can't see into the vault. Can anyone?" The player can prevent that by asking the Moment AI first ("from the doorway, could she see into the vault?"), which answers from the map's sight facts, free and without a roll. Plan conditions can't name things no one could ever perceive (meters, the engine's own bookkeeping); the schema rules them out (`plan_condition`). Party perception rules: `docs/spec/engine.md` §2.
**Why:** Evaluating conditions against the true state let characters act on things nobody could see. Settles the Phase 3 question raised with D35.

### D41. Hidden slack moves deadlines between encounters
**Decided:** The campaign keeps a hidden `slack` value from −2 (tight) to +2 (loose), starting at 0. When an encounter with a deadline ends, the engine compares the ticks to spare with the deadline:
- the fail-forward ending, or 15% or less to spare: slack +1;
- 50% or more to spare: slack −1;
- anything in between: no change.

When the next packet is loaded, each event marked `deadline` moves by 10% of its tick per point of slack, rounded to the nearest tick. Nothing moves once an encounter has started. All thresholds are starting values.
**Why:** A well-run heist earns a tighter next one, and a rough one a little more room, without either running away. It's applied before play, so every deadline is still written down before the encounter starts (`docs/design/encounter-craft.md` §4.5). It's code computing a number between encounters, not a pacing rule inside one.
**Rules out:** Moving a deadline during an encounter; showing slack to the player.

### D42. A step is priced as if its own preconditions hold
**Decided:** When a step's preconditions won't hold at its tick (the key ring is on Snag's belt, not the peg), the engine tells the pricing call to price the action as if they did ("as if: the key ring is on the peg"), in an internal `as_if` list on the step's facts. "Fern, as a rat, pulls the key ring off the peg" is priced as exactly that. The impossibility shows up only in execution, as an interruption (D36).
**Why:** The price is for the action. A band that says "impossible" because of something the card doesn't otherwise mention would be a warning in disguise.

### D43. Slack runs from −10 to +10, in 2% steps
**Decided:** Supersedes the numbers in D41 (its rules stay). Slack runs from −10 to +10, each point moves a deadline by 2% of its tick (at most ±20%), and the total is rounded once to the nearest tick. It still changes by 1 per encounter, so reaching either end takes many encounters.
**Why:** Finer steps and a slower drift, so one good or bad heist nudges the next without a visible swing.

### D46. An interruption happens when someone could first recognize it
**Decided:** The plan stops at the first instant anyone in the party could see or hear that things aren't as the plan expects, not when a character runs into it: the moment the empty peg is in view, not the moment Fern reaches for it. The engine checks before each step and as the actors enter each zone of its route, using the party's pooled perception. The narration gives that moment a few sentences: what they see, how it lands, and where everyone is. Zones can list other zones they see beyond their neighbours (`sees`), so long sightlines are authored, not computed.
**Why:** Noticing the problem only on contact makes characters look oblivious, and wastes moves nobody would have made.

### D47. An interruption needs a step the party can tell will fail
**Decided:** Clarifies D46. The plan stops only when what the party perceives, plus what it knows (learned routines, known events), shows that a *remaining step* won't work. Seeing something different from a snapshot isn't enough. If the plan takes the keys on Snag's Drink and Lyra sees the peg empty during his Pace, nothing stops: the party knows the keys go back on the peg before then. If the step takes them during that Pace, it stops. If the party doesn't know the routine, an empty peg does stop a plan that needs those keys, because from what they know, the plan is broken.
**Why:** D46's wording ("isn't as the plan expects") could stop a well-timed plan at a moment the party fully expected. Recognition means recognizing the plan is in trouble, using everything the party knows.

### D51. A move's path names the zones it enters
**Decided:** Each entry in a `move` or `give` path is one crossing, named by the zone it enters, or by the connection crossed where two connections join the same zones. The last entry is the destination. The engine resolves each crossing to its connection and checks that it exists and that everything crossing it fits (`docs/spec/engine.md` §2). A path has at most two entries; without a path, the destination must be the current zone or next to it.
**Why:** On the bench, all five step calls wrote paths as the zones Fern passed through ("gutter", "guardroom"), never as connection ids. The contract said "id" without saying which kind. The AI's judgment is which way to go; finding the door between two zones is the engine's job (principle 2).
**Rules out:** Requiring connection ids where the zones already say which way.

### D52. State uses the packet's words; anything else is canon
**Decided:** A `set_flag` from any call must use a flag key the packet or a character sheet already uses (in a thing's or connection's flags, a condition, a rule, or a declared ability). The engine rejects any other key and sends it back. A new fact the engine has no word for ("the bars are bent apart") is written as canon with `add_canon`, where the AIs read it. An NPC action that changes nothing the engine tracks (he calls out an offer) has empty `ops`; its `because` says what he does.
**Why:** On the bench, the world call invented flags in every run (`offering_deal`, `with_party`, `wants_deal`) to give an action something to change. Nothing in the engine reads them, so they're invisible state that looks meaningful. Flags only mean something when a condition reads them.
**Rules out:** Free-form flag keys; empty-looking state written to make an action feel concrete.

### D54. The engine finds the way unless the player names it
**Decided:** Refines D51. A step's move or hand-off carries a `path` only when the player said which way ("down the gutter"). Otherwise the step call leaves it out, and the engine takes the shortest route that everyone moving, and everything they carry, fits through. A route longer than two crossings is more than one step, and goes back to the step call.
**Why:** On the bench, when the plan didn't say which way, the step call routed the iron key ring through the rat-sized gutter in most runs, and its retries split the plan into eleven steps. Finding a route that fits is computation (principle 2); which way to go is the player's call (D35).
**Rules out:** The step call choosing routes the player didn't.

### D55. When a named route doesn't fit, the DM rules on it
**Decided:** When a route the player named fails the engine's size check, the engine asks the ruling call one neutral question: can this thing pass through that connection, in this case? It sees the two descriptions, the sizes, who is doing it and the canon so far, and nothing about the plan. A yes becomes a canon ruling, and the engine lets that thing through that connection from then on. A no leaves the step as written, and reality interrupts it when it's reached (D36).
**Why:** Sizes are four coarse categories, and the fiction is finer (a sack of gear can be passed through bars an item at a time). Asking the plan's own calls invites motivated reasoning; a question with no plan attached doesn't. The thing's own description still wins: the Cells' key ring is written as too big for the gutter.
**Rules out:** Treating the size categories as the last word; letting the step or pricing call overrule them.

### D56. The engine opens doors with keys and splits long moves
**Decided:** Someone carrying a connection's `key` unlocks it as they pass through, without a separate effect. A move longer than two crossings is split by the engine into consecutive steps, each inheriting the step's actors, noise and timing, instead of going back to the step call.
**Why:** On the bench, every card 1 run went back for a retry over one of these: "the rogue unlocks the doors" written without the unlock, and "we go" spanning three crossings. Both are bookkeeping, and a retry costs a whole second call.
**Rules out:** Sending mechanical gaps back to the AI when code can close them.

### D57. The ruling call judges the physical situation, not the size categories
**Decided:** Refines D55. The ruling call gets the objects' descriptions and rough real dimensions (a rat-sized grate is about three inches across; cell bars are about five inches apart), never the size categories or "fits" labels, and returns how it would be done when the answer is yes.
**Why:** Given the categories, it repeated them: in three of three runs it ruled the gear sack "too large for bars that only admit small items." The question is whether it would really work, which the categories can't answer.

### D61. NPCs taking part in a plan don't watch it
**Decided:** A step's observers are the NPCs who would see or hear it, except NPCs who act in any step of the same plan. They're with the party for this plan, so they aren't risks the card prices. An NPC the plan leaves out is an observer like any other, and the pricing call judges whether he's a risk.
**Why:** The Cells fixtures never list Brannoc as an observer, though from his cell he sees the corridor Fern runs down. Both cards give him a step (the crusts, walking out). Leaving him off by tier or disposition would be a rule about people; this is a fact about the plan.
**Rules out:** Pricing a plan's own participants as watchers; a packet field that marks NPCs as harmless.

### D62. Recognizing a broken plan takes something seen, not something foreseeable
**Decided:** Clarifies D46 and D47. Before each step and each effect, the engine checks every remaining step that takes an item from where the party last saw it. The plan stops there only if all three hold: the party can see the spot where the item should be; it isn't there; and the routines and events the party knows don't put it back by that step's tick. A problem that's only foreseeable doesn't stop the plan before anyone sees it. Card 1 runs Fern's change on the Drink, even though the party knows the keys go on Snag's belt on his Pace. A problem nobody can see stops the plan at the effect itself.
**Why:** Read literally, D47 would stop card 1 before step 1, because the party knows the routine. But the player committed the plan knowing it too, and D35 keeps a plan's mistakes. The fixture's moment (`card1.results.json`) is when the peg is seen empty. Seeing is what turns a mistake into a recognized one.
**Rules out:** Interrupting a plan for a mistake the party could have deduced but hasn't yet seen; the engine catching a player's mistakes at commit time.

### D63. A key opens a door in passing only when someone needs it open
**Decided:** Refines D56. Someone carrying a connection's key unlocks it as they pass only if something moving with them doesn't fit through it while it's locked. A rat dragging the key ring between the bars leaves the door locked.
**Why:** Unlocking on every pass let Fern unlock the far cell's door while delivering the ring, which set off Snag's `prisoner_loose` trigger a step early. An unlock nobody wrote, and nobody needed, is the step call's job to write, not the engine's.

### D64. Working a door takes hands, from a side it's worked from
**Decided:** Locking, unlocking or otherwise setting a flag on a connection needs one of the step's actors to have hands and stand on a side it can be worked from (`operated_from`, or either side if `reachable_from_other_side`). The same goes for unlocking in passing (D56, D63): whoever carries the key needs hands and must come at it from a workable side. Everyone has hands unless a flag says otherwise; Wild Shape's `declared` block sets `hands: false`, and the packet's rule that ends it gives them back. A step that breaks this isn't sent back to the step call. It's the plan's mistake (D35), and reality interrupts it at that effect (`cant_work`, D36).
**Why:** The designer's ruling: a rat can't turn a key. The engine also wasn't checking `operated_from` at all, though the packet records it for every barrier.
**Rules out:** A rat, or anyone else without hands, unlocking a door; working a barrier from a side it can't be reached from.

### D65. Phase 4 plays the Cells' own party, with relationships held still
**Decided:** The playable Cells use the party the packet places: the player as the rogue, with Fern and Lyra. Setup asks only for a name. Companions talk, take stances and return tagged reactions; the reactions are recorded but not scored, and the relationship tracks stay at their starting values until Phase 5 ports the model.
**Why:** The Cells are built around these three characters' abilities (`fixtures/cells/walkthrough.md`, assumptions). Scoring without the caps, READY and configurations would be half a port. Phase 4 tests the loop, not the romance arc.
**Rules out:** Choosing a class or companions before Phase 5; scoring reactions with part of the model.

### D66. Time spent between plans catches up before a card is made
**Decided:** Checks and conversations add pending ticks. When the player asks for a card, those ticks pass first, one at a time: the world call responds and the narration call tells it. Only then is the plan turned into steps and priced, from that tick. A world turn also follows every executed card and every fight that ends.
**Why:** Pending ticks were applied "at the next world turn", which came after the next plan ran. A plan made after three checks would have started three ticks in the past.
**Rules out:** Pricing a plan from a tick that has already passed.

### D67. In a fight, every declared action gets a band, and the engine deals the damage
**Decided:** Supersedes the moment call's "a band for any unusual action". Each round, the Moment AI gives every action the player declares a kind (strike, ability or other), a target, a band, a reason and both outcomes, and gives each enemy an intent from its sheet, with `yields` when its sheet says it would flee, surrender or bargain. The engine rolls each action against its band; an ability whose conditions are met is automatic. A strike that lands deals 1 damage, or 2 with a real weapon; a strike that misses is hit back for 1. An ability that succeeds takes its target out of the fight. Every enemy nobody engaged hits its target for 1. Enemy HP comes from the sheet or its tier (`content/rules.js`); Gravenhold's DCs aren't used. The fight ends when no enemy is left in it, or when the whole party is down. Ticks accrue on even rounds. Shape: `schemas/calls/moment.js`.
**Why:** One way to roll everywhere (D33). How hard it is to stab a jailer with a bench nail while he swings a club is a judgment. A fixed table from enemy tier to band would make it a lookup.
**Rules out:** DC arithmetic; damage for strikes written by the AI.

### D70. The player chooses who they're talking to
**Decided:** The Moment tab has an addressee: the DM, or a person the party can see or hear. Questions to the DM cost no time. A conversation with a person costs one tick when it ends: when the player turns to someone else, when a check happens, or when a card is made. During a fight, the tab takes the round's actions instead.
**Why:** The time table charged a conversation "1 when it ends" without saying when that is. Asking the AI would be a judgment it has no information for; a choice the player makes is structure.

### D153. The generator, built in one pass
**Decided:** The designer's call (2026-11-06): build the encounter builder in a single pass for the next thread to critique. Code draws a room's exits and its seed (`src/campaign/seed.js`: kind, tone leaning on the leg's strain, two or three motifs from `content/motifs.js`, and for an encounter or the climax an x-factor, shape, what makes its choices meaningful, and one or two factions; the act's used motifs are avoided and reset per act). The sketch call (`complex`) writes the design statement and the sketch; for an encounter or the climax the critique call (`complex`) plays it four ways (force, guile, odd, plain) and, if it finds problems, the sketch call revises once (cut, connect or swap). The encode call (`default`) writes packet data with rough zone sizes and no geometry (`schemas/calls/encode.js`), checked once laid out (`encodeProblems`: the engine's packet checks, mode by kind, exits matching the drawn kinds, a merchant's prices, the act). Code lays it out (`src/engine/layout.js`): zones outward from the party's start, connections on shared walls or squares between levels; a packet it can't lay out ships with no geometry at all, which the packet schema and the engine now allow (D9), and the page shows no map. Pipeline: `generateRoom` in `src/game/generate.js`. Budgets: sketch 32 KB, critique 20 KB, encode 28 KB. One worked example of a finished room's data: `fixtures/generator/encoded.json`. Supersedes the sketch, critique and encode specs' engine-era formats, and the rooms-per-genre seed of D118.
**Why:** The thread holding the Angry GM discussion was nearly out of context; building the whole pipeline while that discussion was fresh, and writing the craft into the repo, keeps it for the next thread to critique, rather than handing over a summary. Every piece that can be code is code (the seed, the checks, the layout); judgment stays in the three calls.
**Rules out:** Coordinates from any AI; a revision that adds elements; a packet with half a map.

### D154. The Forge: the designer's bench for adventures and rooms
**Decided:** The designer's call (2026-11-06), before the generator's stress tests. A bench page, the Forge (`bench/forge.html`, `bench/forge.js`), runs the real pitch call and the real room pipeline so the designer can judge their shape by eye. Its Adventure tab takes two companions and a vibe and shows the world as the DM sees it (place, heart, life, tensions, factions, the five acts with their legs, the player's secrets, features, room kinds, roles, world facts) beside the pitch the player reads. Its Rooms tab, on that world, takes a kind of room, an act, any seed axes the designer pins (tone, x-factor, shape, what makes its choices meaningful, factions, motifs), a brief, and an optional arrival stub, and shows the seed, the sketch, the critique, the revision, and the encoded, laid-out room with its map, stage by stage, with every call's input and output. The pipeline is split for it: `seedRoom` (code) and `designRoom` (the calls and the layout, reporting each stage through `io.onStage`); `generateRoom` is the two in a row. The designer's brief reaches the sketch call only, as its own block after the seed, and only when given; the game never sends one. Every adventure and room, with the designer's notes, is saved to the page's db for the design thread to read.
**Why:** The stress tests ask whether ten sketches read as different and as good as worked ones; before that, the designer needs to see whether the shape is right at all, and to steer a room toward content they have in mind to see how the generator handles it. Splitting the pipeline lets the bench pin seed axes and watch stages without a second copy of it.
**Rules out:** A brief in play; a bench that runs its own copy of the pipeline or prompts.

### D155. The opening shows the situation in motion, and what things are for in the world
**Decided:** The designer's call (2026-11-06), from the first room generated on the Forge. Refines D136. The opening describes what the party takes in after standing safely for a few seconds and watching, not a snapshot: what's happening and who's doing it first, then what touches the party (their own things, anyone looking their way), then the people and what each is busy with, then the ways out, in plain sentences rather than a tour of the floor plan. It may say what a thing is doing and what it's for to the people there ("a siege engine melting a breach in the ice", "a cage of confiscated goods"); it still never says what a thing could do for the party, never gives measurements, and never names anything hidden. Everything usable in sight is still named (`openingMisses` is unchanged), but woven into the situation, not given equal weight in map order. Spec: `docs/spec/packet.md` §5; prompts: sketch and encode; craft §4 and §14.
**Why:** The first generated opening named everything and meant nothing: "beyond", "on the far side", "against the ice wall", "to the north", "overhead", one clause per item, so the player had to map the room on paper and reverse-engineer what was going on. D136's "never what it's for" was written against hints ("rats that work for bread"), but it also banned function in the world, which is exactly what a person standing there would understand in seconds. The line that matters is between what the place is doing and what the party could do with it.
**Rules out:** An opening that lists the room in spatial order; an opening that hides what is plainly going on; an opening that says what a thing could do for the party.

### D156. The sketch goes straight to encode, small, with no route in it
**Decided:** The designer's call (2026-11-06), from the first room generated on the Forge. Supersedes the critique and revision of D120 and D153: generation is the sketch call, then encode, then layout. The critique call, its schema, prompt and spec are removed. The sketch is told to keep rooms small (three or four zones, two or three people who matter, a crowd counting as one, one or two sources of pressure, Act 1 at the low end) in its prompt, not by a check that costs a retry. It never writes a route, not even a golden path as a working note (refines D152's §10). Outcomes are three to five end states (where the party ends up, with what, at what cost), never the plans that got them there. A person's "under pressure" says what they do when the room changes, never their answer to one plan or one companion's ability. The seed no longer offers "a truth to deduce". A call whose connection drops (`upstream_error`) is tried once more (`src/ask.js`), and the Forge can resume a room whose encode failed from its sketch.
**Why:** The critique's most confident finding was that snuffing the braziers empties the priest's threat; the designer's answer is that a player who works that out should be rewarded, so a call spent finding and closing it works against the game. Its other findings mostly pushed the revision to add rules, which the revision did, growing the room. Two complex calls took about two minutes of a five-minute room. The route was the deeper problem the room showed: the sketch planned one way through, then wrote its people around it (a clerk whose one weakness was the way out, a sergeant on the route's schedule, a priest built to catch one companion's ability), so the room read as having expected plans, and its outcomes read as plans. A deduction with one answer is the same answer key by another name. A dropped connection lost a whole room, and the runtime names that code as the one that's transient.
**Rules out:** A review call between sketch and encode; a golden path anywhere; outcomes written as routes; people designed as the key to the room; a size check that costs a call.

### D157. Code draws the kind of heart and the kinds of player secret
**Decided:** The designer's call (2026-11-06), from six worlds pitched on the Forge. Refines D151. Before the pitch call, code draws its seed (`pitchSeed` in `src/campaign/world.js`, from the lists in `content/pitch.js`): what kind of thing the heart is (a being, an object, a door, a bargain, a person, a working, a place, a truth) and two different kinds of player secret (something they did here, someone here who knows them, why they really came, something they've carried, a false memory, a debt, a tie of blood or birth, what the heart wants from them). A reroll draws a new seed. The world has two player secrets, one of each kind, tied to the place but not necessarily the heart. The pitch no longer ends on what's stirring in the depths. The player is always "they": the pitch prompt says so, and Gravenhold's secrets are rewritten from "he".
**Why:** All six worlds had a vast ancient being asleep at the bottom, a secret where the seal answers to the player's blood, and a secret where the thing below dreams of the player "more clearly the deeper they go", which is Gravenhold's first two secrets nearly word for word, and every pitch ended on something below breathing or humming. The prompt asked for exactly that ("sealed, sleeping or hidden"; "tie the player to the heart"; "what's stirring in its depths"), and the worked example showed one answer. Code picking the question is how rooms already avoid the average one (D118). One world called the player "he", copied from Gravenhold.
**Rules out:** A heart or player secrets the model chooses the kind of; three secrets that all make the player chosen; gendering the player.

### D158. The seed says whose room it is, and most rooms are no one's
**Decided:** The designer's call (2026-11-06), from the Forge. Each room's seed names whose room it is: one companion's slot, or no one's. No one's is about half; a companion never has two rooms in a row; the climax is both companions' (D148). Only that companion's story goes to the sketch (the features it needs, and her quest when its act is near); both companions' values and abilities still do, so each reacts as herself. "A companion's thing" is drawn only when the room is someone's. Nothing a companion needs can be failed in a calm room. The Forge can pin it. Code: `src/campaign/seed.js`, `designParty` in `src/context.js`.
**Why:** The sketch was sent both companions' stories every time, and asked to touch one "where it fits", so two of four rooms gave both companions a goal and one made a companion's hunger an outcome a rest room could fail. The designer's question: does every room need both? A companion's thread lands harder when it's that room's, and an ordinary room lets both of them simply be at the table.
**Rules out:** Every room touching both companions' stories; a companion's thread in two rooms running; a companion need that a calm room can fail.

### D159. Openings in paragraphs, fewer things, and rooms not shaped around one ability
**Decided:** The designer's call (2026-11-06), from the Forge. Refines D155 and D156. The opening is two or three short paragraphs, each about one thing rather than one part of the floor: what's happening and who's doing it; what the party wants and who has it; what's coming and the ways on. Each thing in sight goes in the paragraph it belongs to, never in a closing sweep of what's left; set dressing is left out. A room has at most five or six things, counting only what someone could take, use, break or bargain over. The encode call makes a crowd or squad one entry, and its notes never say how something answers one party member's ability. The sketch never places a prop for one party member's ability or adds a detail to block one; a constraint the place has anyway is fine. Plumbing: a reply with no JSON is tried once more, with what it said kept in the call record (`noJson`); the Forge never sends or shows a pin the kind of room or the world can't take.
**Why:** The new openings started in motion and then swept up the rest in floor-plan order ("centre stage… overhead… upstage… across the stage"); the one that didn't, the bazaar's, was grouped by meaning. Every room had 10 to 13 things against "a handful", and every named thing lengthens the opening. The encode split a squad into identical entries. The stage had "no soil anywhere" and an orange tree in a tub, a key cut for one companion, and the garden's notes said how the bees answer another's ability. One encode came back with no JSON, and the reply was thrown away, so its cause is unknown. Motifs pinned for a quiet room rode invisibly into an encounter.
**Rules out:** An opening that tours the floor plan; floors, lamps and walls as things; a room built around, or against, one party member's ability.

### D160. The sketch is a DM's prep note, premise first, steered by form and examples rather than rules
**Decided:** The designer's call (2026-11-07), from a discussion of what plays in theatre of the mind and the Forge's first encounter rooms, as the first of several iterations. Refines D152, D153, D156 and D159. The sketch call writes a short prep note in this order: a premise (the room in one sentence a player would repeat), a shape (one picturable shape and where the party, the danger and the ways on sit on it, in relational words, no measurements), a line for each thing (what it is, what state it's in, where it is next to the others, never what it's for), a cut (the weakest thing, named and left out), the pressure (what's already moving), three to five outcomes, notes for the DM (wants, what the place won't allow, anything worth having, the ways on) and the opening, which now shows how the room works through a small event in its first seconds. It replaces the design statement (start, goal, the challenge as "X by Y, hard because Z") and the sectioned prose. The prompt steers by form, order, questions the model asks itself and never writes down, and a pulp tone, not by rules. The sketch sees three example notes (`fixtures/generator/sketches.json`: the watchtower camp, the winery, the ringed shaft) with no commentary, instead of the encoded packet, which encode still sees. The seed is split into what it fixes (kind, tone, whose room, the ways on) and leanings the sketch uses or leaves: motifs (one or two for an encounter, down from two or three), what the room is built around (a place at its own work, one big thing in a space, a danger in the way), a picturable shape (which replaces linear, branching, open and hub), a prize (none about half the time, or something worth the risk), the x-factor and factions; "what makes its choices meaningful" is no longer drawn. Encode adds nothing the note left out and fills people's inner fields with the plainest short reading of what the note shows, never a secret or lever. The craft's §16 holds the discussion's lessons and governs where earlier sections pull toward more. The Forge pins the new axes and shows the note.
**Why:** The Forge's encounter rooms each held one excellent rule (the brazier line, a show that runs on cue before a crowd, silk a watcher feels) buried under fifteen to twenty things, most of them spawned by requirements: a required second side became a second faction in all three; seven required inner-life fields per person became single levers; the challenge formula wrote approaches into the design; measured blockouts and hidden one-use keys (the knife that cuts silk silently) asked for precision and answer keys the medium can't carry. Asking a model to make its elements interact gets it to write the interactions down, and a written interaction is an expected solution; hard rules and rejecting checks would be the competing-rules problem again (principle 1). Form, order and unexplained examples give the model the ingredients of interaction (states that can tip, things placed near each other) and nowhere to write a solution.
**Rules out:** The design statement and challenge formula in the sketch; motifs, a second side or "meaningful by" as requirements; a worked example the sketch learns from with commentary attached; checks that reject a sketch for its count of things.

### D161. The sketch sees no example rooms; the place must work, and nothing is a machine with a rule
**Decided:** The designer's call (2026-11-07), from the first three prep-note rooms on the Forge. Refines D160. The sketch call is sent no example rooms; `fixtures/generator/sketches.json` stays as worked notes for designers, referenced by the craft. The shape now says how the people there use the space, so the layout follows from what the place does, and gives more than one way past the danger. The premise is one short sentence with one idea. Notes say what people want and fear, never how their senses work or a rule a player must learn. The opening's small event shows what's dangerous, not how the room works. Two unwritten questions are added: could a player sketch the room on a napkin after hearing it once, and does anything ask a player to balance, time, count or track who sees what. The seed's shapes drop "a long hall" and "a crossroads" and add terraces and a cage hung in the air.
**Why:** All three rooms copied the examples: goblins on shift, a boss watching, a prize at the boss's feet, with the examples' phrases ("want their shift over", "nobody the wiser", "to be sold") back word for word; the designer's experience is that any example becomes an anchor. The boil hall placed things without saying how the place worked, so it couldn't be pictured. The see-saw bridge was a machine with a rule to balance in real time, and the Boilmaster a system of sight lines, which the prompt's "shows how the room works" and "what the place won't allow" invited. The bowl, with two sides built into its premise and a picturable shape, played best, but its three-part premise left the goal unclear.
**Rules out:** Example rooms in the sketch's input; a layout of positions with no reason; rooms built on a mechanism, a sense rule or real-time balancing.

### D162. Rooms vary by what's happening and whose place it is, and people's attention is said plainly
**Decided:** The designer's call (2026-11-07), from the second three prep-note rooms on the Forge. Refines D160 and D161. The seed fixes what the place is busy with (labour, a feast, a rite, a punishment, trade, getting ready for a raid, the night watch, sickness, ruin, beasts, a burial, a contest, building, drill), drawn by code and never repeated within an act, and, for an encounter or the climax, whose place it is: one of the world's factions, no longer one or two as a leaning. The sketch fits "busy with" to the world even when the world is famous for something else. The campaign keeps each generated room's premise (`camp.premises`, reset with the act), and the sketch sees the act's earlier premises and makes this room a different kind of place; the Forge passes the premises of earlier rooms on the same adventure and act. People's lines say what they're busy with and where their eyes are; notes say what pulls their attention away; an unwritten question asks where a player would try to sneak and what's looking there. Encode keeps zones to where a different decision happens and things to the note's own list. The offer motif keeps its stated terms: an NPC's own plan is his idea, offered in the fiction, not the party's route.
**Why:** All three rooms were the same oil works (a boiling vat, a bugbear boss, a captive at a wheel, a horn), and the examples' phrases outlived the examples: the world's life opens on boiling the spring into oil and Act 2 lists the pumps, so with the examples gone the world became the anchor, and "a place at its own work" picked its most famous work every time. Any salient context anchors, so variety has to come from code. Every room was the ruling faction whatever the seed named. With sense rules removed, nothing said where anyone was looking, and two bosses "watched everything", leaving the designer no way to start sneaking. Encode made each of five terraces a zone and each tool a thing.
**Rules out:** Variety left to the model; factions as a leaning; a watcher with no gaps; a zone per part of the shape.

### D163. The sketch prompt holds no images; code draws the shape and its centrepiece; a climax is a showdown
**Decided:** The designer's call (2026-11-07), from seven Forge rooms across three worlds. Refines D160–D162. Every concrete image is taken out of the sketch prompt, including those that only illustrated an instruction; concrete content reaches the sketch only through lists code draws and rotates. The seed fixes the space's shape, from a wider list with more horizontal and irregular spaces, and its centrepiece, the one thing the eye goes to; neither repeats within an act (`camp.usedShapes`, `camp.usedCentres`), and "a cage or frame hung in the air" is gone. Pressure is never a count of steps, and an unwritten question asks for the second idea, not the first. A climax gets its own block (the act's goal, the boss of the faction whose place it is, what that faction wants and how it behaves, the heart, and the act's rooms so far) and a section of the prompt: an antagonist present and acting, changing the room in turns like a boss fight; the act's rooms paying off; a choice at the peak; bigger but still legible. The Forge pins shape and centrepiece for every kind of room.
**Why:** Six of seven rooms, in three worlds, were a captive lowered toward doom a notch at a time: the prompt's own "hanging by a thread", "the figure lowering herself toward the child", "sinking" and the pincer-mouthed girl, and the shape list's hung cage, came back, and the device ticks every instruction at once. Four rooms were a round shaft with a ring gallery, from a shape list heavy with round, vertical spaces; the designer proposed a shape plus a centrepiece. A climax with nothing to say it was one read as an intense room rather than a boss fight; the two that worked had a brief asking for an epic fight, or the act's threads converging.
**Rules out:** Concrete images in the sketch prompt; shape as a leaning; a climax that is only a harder room.

### D164. Generation in three stages: the world once, the act when it begins, the room when it's entered
**Decided:** The designer's call (2026-11-08), after a review of what is decided at each of the three moments the game generates, and the designer's notes on it. Built on a separate branch (`claude/three-stage-calls`) so the D163 Forge stays as it plays. Refines D150, D151, D153, D157, D162 and D163.
- **The world (pitch call, once)** keeps the place, way out, roles, heart, life, tensions, features, the player's secrets and invariants, and replaces the five acts with five **regions** (name, what it is, sanctuary, the factions that hold it, how it feels) on a stated rising curve: personal, communal, ancient, mythic, then aftermath. Each faction gets a **ladder** of three named leaders with visible hooks, and `boss` goes. Two to four **mysteries** each hold a truth that is never confirmed, an **edge** (from which act, at that act's climax or in one faction's rooms, what becomes more likely to work), and three to five clues, each the words or picture alone, part of the truth said nearly outright. Gone: `acts` with their `mechanics` and template goals, `roomKinds`, `openAct`, "and its records" from the master role and "their alarm" from faction rules. The act jobs live in `content/setting.js` (`arc`). The pitch can run without the Gravenhold example, for an A/B on the Forge. Gravenhold is rewritten to the shape.
- **The act call (new, once per act, and per post-game act with the player's intent)** reads the world, the act's job and scale, each earlier act's peak, premises and log lines, and the peaks used, and writes the act's goal in the world's terms, what the region is like now, who holds it, at least four busy lines in the region's words, the **peak** (chosen by whose design blocks the party, never by who the party likes, climbing the ladders, never repeated) with how it acts in turns, and two or three visible signs of it. It runs during the previous sanctuary, and Act 1's once the world is accepted.
- **The room** draws busy-ness and its owner from the act's plan; its prize from none, gold, a relic or a **clue** (one the campaign hasn't placed, with a carrier code draws); and **at most one extra**, never two rooms running: someone from before (only in an encounter or an oddity, once an act each), or a sign of the peak (one a leg). The sketch no longer sees abilities, the heart or the tensions outside a climax, every faction, or room kinds; it sees the region now and its owner's rules and local face. Its x-factor and core leanings go, and it keeps one motif. A climax stands the act's peak at its centre. Code adds the mysteries' edges that apply to a packet as hidden facts after encode; the sketch never sees them.
- **Between rooms:** a packet thing may carry a clue as its reward, found by holding it or, when `fixed`, by being in plain sight. The verdict names up to two people worth meeting again, which the campaign keeps (the last 30). The campaign keeps clues found and placed, each act's history, and the act's plan. The campfire is given the words of the clues found, to wonder about. The player's secrets and the rings stay story reveals by act.
**Why:** The worlds copied Gravenhold's act text almost word for word (every Act 1 goal was "escape the cell block, recover some gear, learn who your companions are"), and `mechanics` carried the engine era's stealth-simulation vocabulary (tally counts, alarm networks) into rooms; `roomKinds`' examples anchored rooms (bugbears). Peaks fixed at world generation would make the party fight a faction it had allied with, and Act 5 could never answer Act 4. Any pool a room sees becomes an anchor, so later stages get slices chosen by code. The designer's notes set the rest: clues are rewards, not deliveries, and blatant (a journal page, not a breath that doesn't fog); mysteries are lore that might give an edge, never a puzzle the DM confirms; missing a clue is fine; alliances are fragile and personal, so a returning person matters more than a faction score; abilities removed from the sketch end rooms built around one.
**Rules out:** World-written act content; a peak chosen before the act; faction standing as a score; a DM, companion or reveal confirming a mystery; a clue as a subtle detail; any pool sent whole to a room.

### D165. Only the seed's prize is a reward; mysteries never cover the rings or the player's secrets
**Decided:** The designer's call (2026-11-08), from the first A/B of worlds with and without the Gravenhold example. Refines D164. Encode is told the room's only prize and the check sends back any other reward: a clue the seed didn't place, or a second prize. The sketch is told only a clue says anything about the place's lore. The pitch prompt drops its illustrations of what a mystery might be about, and says mysteries are never about the rings or the player's secrets. The Forge can reuse the last pitch's seed, so a world with the example and one without answer the same question.
**Why:** One room turned its relic into a tablet reciting lore, and encode gave it a clue id the world doesn't hold; another added a second prize the seed never drew. Both worlds wrote a "how the heart was sealed" and a "faction bound by an oath" mystery, the prompt's own two illustrations; one wrote a mystery explaining the rings, which are a story reveal. The two worlds drew different seeds, so the A/B couldn't separate the example from the seed.
**Rules out:** Rewards the seed didn't draw; lore in a non-clue prize; a mystery about the rings or the player's secrets.

### D166. Worlds are written without the example; the act call tidies what it can; no chosen-one secrets
**Decided:** The designer's call (2026-11-08), from a fair A/B (the same vibe and seed, with and without Gravenhold as the worked example). Refines D157 and D164. The pitch no longer sees Gravenhold unless the Forge asks (`withExample`, off by default). The pitch prompt adds what the example used to teach: roles are short phrases, never names; no world fact states a mystery's truth; no edge depends on timing, counting or balance. The secret kinds "a tie of blood or birth" and "what the heart wants from the player" become "someone the player loves, held or working in the place" and "a rival or enemy from the player's past risen to power here". The act call names busy kinds as free text from a list in its input, and code matches them (`tidyAct`), drops unknown holders, and sends back only an act with fewer than two usable lines; a failed act call is kept on the Forge. On the Forge, a brief comes first where it and the seed disagree.
**Why:** With the example, the world copied Gravenhold's sanctuaries ("a ledge… real stars", "a cooling-cistern"), escape, heartbeat fact and its oath mystery, as it had in the first test; without it, the world was more original and more cohesive (a festival every faction converges on), and its slips (named roles, a fact giving a mystery away, a timing edge) were the prompt's to close. Both worlds made the player a chosen one of the builders' blood: the seed list asked for it. "Begin act 1" failed on the Forge, and failures weren't saved; the strictest part of the act call was an exact-match list for busy kinds and holders, which a loosely worded reply failed twice. A brief for an epic duel was bent into a feast by an unpinned busy-ness.
**Rules out:** The worked example by default; an exact-match list the model must reproduce; chosen-one secret kinds.

### D167. The act call gives rooms abstract direction and pools dealt one at a time, never a picture
**Decided:** The designer's call (2026-11-08), after comparing the three-stage Forge's rooms with the D163 Forge's in one world. Refines D164. Whatever the act call writes that reaches more than one room is abstract: the act's **question** (what it's about) and **aim** (what the party should come away knowing or holding). Whatever is concrete is a pool code deals out one item to one room, each used once in the act: busy lines (each different people in a different kind of place), **locals** (five to eight inhabitants of no side, which replace the motif when an act has them), **settings** for quiet rooms, oddities and campfires (a different one from earlier in the act until they run out), **news** of what earlier acts set in motion (a third kind of extra), and the peak's signs. The region-now paragraph goes, replaced by **state** (what has changed), which only the climax and the next act read. The act also picks the one or two **live mysteries** whose clues its rooms hold, and the remembered people likely to turn up. A room gets no local face when that face is the act's peak. A companion's story feature reaches the sketch once an act. The tone darkens as the act goes on. When one way on leads to an encounter and another to a calm room, the encounter's is the easier to reach (the sketch prompt and the craft). No mood words: the designer's call.
**Why:** The act's vivid region paragraph reached every room, and both rooms built themselves from it: stone-making lens light in each, a cracked lens in each, the same grey-bearded dwarf foreman twice, dancers for Nadia twice, while the D163 Forge's rooms, built from abstract constraints, were different from each other and charming (a flock of tiny eyeballs grazed by blind shepherds). The act's peak was its faction's lowest rung, which rooms receive as the local face, so it appeared in person. A world fact made the danger a rule about sight, and rooms followed it into sliding pools of light. Concrete material shared by every room anchors; dealt one at a time, it varies. Calm rooms benefit from a strong setting to anchor on, as long as each is different.
**Rules out:** A picture of the region sent to every room; mood words; the act's peak in person before the climax; a companion's same feature in two rooms of an act.

### D168. Clues are journal pages read at camp; motifs return without examples; one strange thing a room
**Decided:** The designer's call (2026-11-08), from an act and three rooms on the three-stage Forge. Refines D164 and D167. Each mystery holds a **journal**: who wrote it, and the journal in order, torn into three to five pages, with what the truth needs spread across them and each page plainly saying what its writer knew. A page is a prize like gold ("a journal page" replaces "a clue"; the carriers list goes): the sketch is told only who wrote it, never its words; encode makes it a thing the party can carry, described but never transcribed; the party reads it at camp, where the campfire sees each page with its writer. The act call's **locals** go, and rooms take one **motif** again, now a situation's shape with its examples stripped out. The sketch's tone is grounded: one strange thing a room, at most, and everything else what you'd expect in such a place. The act's busy lines are the place's ordinary business, not curiosities.
**Why:** The rooms had started to read as gonzo again: each stacked a quirky premise (the act's busy lines had become curiosities, like a sergeant reading dreams aloud), a quirky local the act had been asked to make "odd, specific, memorable" (a rat-catcher whose rat is his late wife, a narrating halfling, a pebble-trading monk), and quirky clue-givers (a dwarf shouting half a sentence of lore). Clues as speech or scratched walls put lore into the room's mouth; pages read at camp keep it out of the room, and a journal written whole holds together better than scattered fragments. What worked held: the easier way on led to the encounter in all three rooms, the peak's local face stayed out, the signs of the peak landed, and calm-room settings were strong.
**Rules out:** Locals; clue carriers other than journal pages; a page's words in the room; stacked strangeness.

### D169. Rooms are generated D163-style; the act decides what comes up, not what rooms hold; worn-out props are retired
**Decided:** The designer's call (2026-11-08), after the D168 round. Refines D164, D167 and D168. Encounter rooms are generated as the D163 Forge did: the seed's busy-ness is a generic kind again, its leanings are back (what the room is built around, the x-factor, one or two motifs), and the sketch sees the world's place, life, heart and tensions and the owning faction whole, with the D163 tone and no "one strange thing" rule. What stays from D164 on is structural: no ability text, journal pages read at camp, the peak only at the climax and as rationed signs, rationed extras, calm-room settings. The act call stops writing busy lines and decides what should come up during the act: a general **setting** (what this part of the place is and who's in it, never particular rooms or sights), the question and aim, holders, each companion's **thread** (what of her story comes up, which her room reads once an act), calm-room settings, news, live mysteries, returning people, and the peak with its signs; it sees the companions. Each room sees **the way so far** this act, in order, by kind and premise, and follows on from it. The room before a campfire, the climax or the sanctuary has one way on, to it. **Worn-out props** (`content/stale.js`: hanging cages; ledgers, tallies, counts and clerks keeping records) go to every seed, the act and the pitch as never to use, and a sketch that uses one goes back once (`sketchProblems`).
**Why:** The designer preferred the D163 rooms' charm and originality; every way the act tried to shape rooms (a region picture, busy lines, locals, a quirk limit) either anchored them or flattened them. The act's real job is what the room call can't know: what came before, what lies ahead, and what should come up in between. Campfires were already guaranteed by the leg count; the room before one had two exits to the same campfire. Hanging cages and ledgers or counting survived every prompt change, so they're named and checked.
**Rules out:** Busy lines from the act; the act shaping a room's contents; a room before a campfire with two ways on; hanging cages, ledgers and counting.
