# Encounter design: notes for the rethink

Input for Phase 7's step 0 (D139), not a design. Written after the first table playtest, for the thread that rethinks what makes a good encounter now that the DM rules. When the thread settles its answers, they go into `docs/design/encounter-craft.md` and this file goes.

---

## 1. What the playtest showed about the Cells

The Cells was built when the engine judged outcomes: a stealth puzzle the engine could check. Played with the DM ruling, it was solved at tick 11 of 32, and the danger was gone after the second plan.

- **Its properties were answers.** The key ring's entry says "several rats can carry it quietly"; the bucket's says dumping it "floods the drain and Snag spends a tick cleaning up." Those aren't properties, they're plans already ruled on. The winning plan felt clever, but the packet had written down that it works.
- **One job per cell** ("physical work, talking, or seeing") is an answer key handed out by person. When a plan didn't need a slot, that companion had nothing to do: Nadia's abilities never mattered.
- **One of everything:** one antagonist, one alarm, one deadline. Gnawing the drum removed the only alarm, so Snag had no move left, and the last six plans were mop-up on easy rolls. Uzga's deadline never came into play.
- **A precise routine turned planning into arithmetic.** Most of the companion talk was working out which beat the ring is on the peg. That's where the companions got facts wrong, and it's the engine's job, not theirs (principle 2).
- **The packet decided in advance who can act.** The rats were a thing, so the card that made them act went back twice. Open-ended play will recruit, charm and bribe things nobody planned for.

**What worked, and why it matters:**
- **The best moments came from a person, not the mechanism.** Snag haggling, "magic did it", and his surrender came from his sheet (his fear of Uzga) and the DM's judgment.
- **The sharpest beat came from structure that creates pressure.** Snag's routine found the empty peg and stopped the plan at a moment the player could plan from.

So the line isn't structure against openness. It's **structure that creates pressure** against **structure that pre-solves**.

## 2. The shift

The engine era designed **a mechanism the player solves**: the encounter is a lock, and the packet lists how the lock behaves. The DM era should design **a situation the DM runs**: people who want things, in a place with real constraints, under pressure that moves on its own. The packet's job is to give the DM's judgment good material, never to have judged already. "The judgment is the product" (`docs/principles.md`) applies to encounters as much as to calls.

## 3. Candidate principles

Starting points for the thread to keep, cut or replace. None is settled.

1. **Situations, not mechanisms.** An encounter is people wanting things, under pressure, in a place. Locks, routines and alarms support that; they aren't the encounter.
2. **Say what a thing is, never what it's for.** Material, size, state, who cares about it: "iron, six keys, heavy." What it lets you do is a ruling the DM makes when a plan arrives (principle 7's third layer). A test: could this sentence be read as a hint? Then it's an answer.
3. **Pre-rulings may close doors, never open them.** "Iron, so nature magic can't touch it" makes a problem. "Several rats can carry it quietly" solves one.
4. **Resistance comes from people.** A lock resists once. A person adapts, bargains, panics, lies, wants something. Every encounter needs people whose wants press on the party, and the DM needs their wants and fears more than their stats.
5. **At least two sides that want different things from each other,** not just from the party. That's where leverage comes from (play one against the other) and where emergence comes from without the DM inventing anything (principle 8): their wants interacting is the surprise.
6. **No single point of failure for the danger.** If removing one element empties the threat, the encounter is a puzzle with one key. Pressure should come in more than one kind (people, time, place, cost), so a clever bypass changes the situation rather than ending it.
7. **A good solution creates the next problem.** Success should move the situation, not end it: the guard is tied up, but now someone's missing him; the keys are taken, so the cells are a crime scene. This is D131's "the party always moves on" applied inside the encounter.
8. **Pressure, not timetables.** Time should push (something arrives, something closes, someone loses patience), not be a schedule to compute. If the player needs arithmetic to use it, it's the engine's job, shown as a fact.
9. **Everyone has work, through properties, not slots.** Abilities should touch the world because the world has properties they can grab, not because a task was assigned to each character.
10. **Few elements, richly connected.** Four things that each touch two others beat twelve inert ones. Interlock (encounter-craft §3.3) survives; exhaustive contents don't.
11. **Stakes beyond succeed or fail.** What it costs, who gets saved or left behind, what the party has to choose between. The gear sack (an optional goal) and the miner's ultimatum were the Cells' best stakes because they were choices, not obstacles.
12. **The companions belong to the situation.** Each companion's wants, limits and tie can meet the encounter: Fern's "if the rats get hurt, I'm out" was a stance the situation earned. An encounter that touches a companion's values gives the romance pillar something to do between camps.

## 4. The opposite failure

Open-ended encounters run by an AI DM drift toward **yes-and mush**: nothing resists, every plan works, nothing is at stake, and the DM narrates success after success. Principle 8 still holds: "emergent" has to mean placed elements interacting, never the DM inventing a twist. The rigid Cells at least pushed back. Whatever replaces rigidity as the source of resistance (people, cost, pressure, hard material facts) has to be designed in, because the DM won't supply it unprompted.

## 5. Questions for the thread

- **What is an encounter, now?** A place, a situation, a scene? How big, and where does one end (the encounter is still the unit of complete knowledge, principle 4)?
- **Does tick-level time still earn its place?** The Cells' 32-tick clock never mattered. What does the DM need from time: a clock it can read, or just events and their order?
- **What must a packet carry,** and what should it leave out so the DM rules? Routines, sheets, notes on reactions, hidden things, fixed facts: which of these create pressure, and which pre-solve?
- **The heist and the cavern.** What in them is about the situation (two clocks; places where different spaces meet; a disguise that helps with one side and gives you away to the other; exits that close as the alarm rises; an ending that's a choice) and what was engine-era simulation (senses for every creature, exact patrols)?
- **Genres.** Escape, infiltration, negotiation with a deadline, a hazard, a fight with a setup: are these still the right kinds, or are they mechanism kinds that should become situation kinds?
- **How does the critique call judge an encounter?** Candidate checks from §3: one bypass empties the threat; a property reads as a hint; only one side wants anything; time needs arithmetic; a companion has nothing in it.
- **How much is prepared?** "Prepare situations and purposes, not simulations" (`docs/principles.md`). Where's the line for a generated packet?
